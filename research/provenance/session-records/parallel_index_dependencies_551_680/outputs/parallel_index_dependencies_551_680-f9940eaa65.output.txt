two-bit-row-restriction-dimension  1. A larger multiplier space has the required restriction dimension 
   Let \(v=m\ell\), and let \(\mathcal A_{2,k}\) be the homogeneous degree-\(k\) space of squarefree bit polynomials using at most two bits in each row. Suppose \(1\le k\le v\), and put 
   \[
    \varepsilon=
      m\binom{\ell}{3}\frac{\binom{v-3}{k-3}}{\binom vk},
  \] 
   with \(\varepsilon=0\) if \(k&lt;3\) or \(\ell&lt;3\). Every excluded \(k\)-set of bits contains a triple from one row. A union bound over these triples therefore gives 
   \[
      \dim\mathcal A_{2,k}\ge(1-\varepsilon)\binom vk.
      \tag{CA-dimension}
  \] 
   For an affine flat of codimension \(r\), ordinary polynomial restriction has image dimension at most \(\binom{v-r+k}{k}\). If \(r\ge2k+3\), then 
   \[
    \frac{\binom{v-r+k}{k}}{\binom vk}
       =\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{r-k}{v-j}\right)
       \le \exp\!\left(-\frac{k(r-k)}v\right)
       \le e^{-k^2/v}.
       \tag{CA-restriction}
  \] 
   Thus, if \(k=\lceil\sqrt{v\ln(4M)}\rceil\), \(\varepsilon\le1/2\), and there are at most \(M\) such flats, the sum of their restriction-image dimensions is at most \(\frac14\binom vk&lt;\dim\mathcal A_{2,k}\). A common nonzero kernel polynomial exists over every field. The restrictions here are ordinary polynomials, so the existing affine-coordinate argument supplies literal ideal representations with coefficient degree at most \(k-1\). If no such flats exist, the kernel assertion is immediate.

### 558 proof:chessboard-homology-expansion
whitepaper-chessboard-homology-expansion  2. Inline homological background added for the manuscript 
   This is an exposition of the classical  BLVZ chessboard bound , not a new connectivity theorem. Work with reduced homology over \(\mathbb F_2\). Call a complex \(q\)-acyclic when its reduced homology vanishes from degree \(-1\) through \(q\); \((-1)\)-acyclic means it has a vertex, and conditions below \(-1\) are vacuous. 
    Homological cover lemma.  Suppose a finite complex \(K\) is covered by subcomplexes \(L_1,\ldots,L_t\), their nerve is \(q\)-acyclic, and every nonempty intersection of \(s\) members is \((q-s+1)\)-acyclic, where \(q\ge0\). Then \(K\) is \(q\)-acyclic. 
    Proof.  Use the finite double complex 
   \[
    D_{a,b}=
      \bigoplus_{\substack{J\subseteq[t],\ |J|=a+1\\L_J\ne\varnothing}}
           C_b(L_J;\mathbb F_2),
       \qquad L_J=\bigcap_{j\in J}L_j,\quad a,b\ge0.
  \] 
   The vertical differential is simplicial boundary; the horizontal differential omits a cover index. Their sum squares to zero in characteristic two. Augment horizontal rows to \(C_b(K)\). For any fixed simplex of \(K\), the indices of cover members containing it form a nonempty simplex, whose augmented chain complex is exact. Taking the direct sum over simplices shows that the horizontal rows are exact after augmentation. Computing horizontally first therefore recovers the homology of \(K\). 
   Vertically first, the first page is \(\bigoplus_{|J|=a+1}H_b(L_J)\). Its groups with \(b&gt;0\), \(a+b\le q\), vanish by the intersection hypotheses. For \(b=0\), \(a\le q\), every nonempty intersection is connected, so the remaining horizontal groups and maps are the nerve chain complex. At \(a=q+1\), the augmentation \(H_0(L_J)\to\mathbb F_2\) is still onto; hence the incoming boundaries contain all nerve boundaries needed in degree \(q\). The second page has zero reduced groups in total degrees at most \(q\). Later pages cannot create nonzero groups from zero entries, and the finite filtration converges. Augmenting degree zero gives the asserted reduced-homology vanishing. \(\square\) 
    Homological chessboard bound.  For \(a,b\ge1\), define 
   \[
    \nu(a,b)=\min\left\{a,b,
          \left\lfloor\frac{a+b+1}{3}\right\rfloor\right\}.
  \] 
   The chessboard complex \(\Delta_{a,b}\) is \((\nu(a,b)-2)\)-acyclic. In particular \(b\ge2a-1\) implies \(\widetilde H_{a-2}(\Delta_{a,b};\mathbb F_2)=0\). 
    Proof by the standard star cover.  Transpose so \(a\le b\) and induct on \(a\). If \(\nu=1\), nonemptiness suffices. Otherwise \(q=\nu-2\ge0\) and \(a\ge2\). Cover by the closed stars of the row-one vertices \((1,j)\). They are cones. A matching avoiding row one uses at most \(a-1&lt;b\) columns, so it belongs to some star. An intersection of \(s\ge2\) stars is \(\Delta_{a-1,b-s}\), on the remaining rows and columns. Every proper subfamily has a vertex in its intersection, while the full intersection does not. The nerve is the boundary of a \((b-1)\)-simplex, a sphere of dimension \(b-2\). 
   Here \(\nu\le b-1\), so the nerve is \(q\)-acyclic. For a nonempty \(s\)-fold intersection, \(s\ge2\), let \(\mu=\nu(a-1,b-s)\). Each entry in this minimum is at least \(\nu-s+1\): 
   \[
     a-1\ge\nu-s+1,\qquad b-s\ge\nu-s+1,\qquad
     a+b-s\ge3\nu-s-1\ge3(\nu-s+1).
  \] 
   Thus \(\mu\ge\nu-s+1\). Induction, transposing the smaller board if needed, gives \((q-s+1)\)-acyclicity of the intersection. Apply the cover lemma. Finally \(b\ge2a-1\) makes \(\nu=a\), giving the exact consequence used by the matching-moment argument. \(\square\)
