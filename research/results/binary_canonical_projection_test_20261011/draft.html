Permutation indicator component

Lemma. On an \(r\times r\) array \(X=(x_{ij})\), let \(\Phi_r\) assert exactly one one in every row and column: one positive clause for each row and column and pairwise negative clauses within each. It has \(O(r^3)\) literal occurrences. Let \(H_r\) be its written satisfaction product and \(I_r=\operatorname{ML}(H_r)\). Over any field, \[I_r(X)=\sum_{\pi\in S_r}\prod_i x_{i,\pi(i)} \prod_{(i,j):j\ne\pi(i)}(1-x_{ij}),\] so \(\deg I_r\le r^2\), all components below degree \(r\) vanish, and its degree-\(r\) homogeneous component is \(\operatorname{perm}_r(X)\). In characteristic two this is \(\det_r(X)\). If a size-\(S\), depth-\(d\) circuit computes \(I_r\) over a field \(k\), and an extension \(E\) contains \(r^2+1\) distinct elements, interpolation constructs a circuit over \(E\) for that component of size \(O((r^2+1)(S+r^2+1))\) and depth at most \(d+3\).

Proof. Each displayed term is the Boolean point indicator of one permutation matrix. The sum is multilinear, is one precisely at satisfying assignments and zero elsewhere; multilinear uniqueness on the Boolean cube proves the identity. Each term has a degree-\(r\) product of selected entries times factors with constant term one. The resulting distinct degree-\(r\) monomials sum to the permanent. No cancellation can introduce lower-degree terms. The sign in a determinant is immaterial in characteristic two.

Choose distinct \(\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_{r^2}\in E\). Invert the Vandermonde matrix to obtain constants \(\lambda_a\in E\) extracting the coefficient of \(t^r\) from every polynomial of degree at most \(r^2\). Then \[\operatorname{perm}_r(X)=\sum_{a=0}^{r^2}\lambda_a I_r(\alpha_aX).\] Use copies of the supplied circuit, one input-scaling layer, one output-scaling layer and one sum layer. The stated size includes scaled inputs and wires. This is nonuniform arithmetic circuit existence; it does not assert uniform construction of the original circuit. For \(k=\mathbb F_2\), an extension \(\mathbb F_{2^e}\) with \(e=\lceil\log_2(r^2+1)\rceil\) suffices. No return to binary constants or fixed-field bounded-depth circuitry is included. Even \(t^2-t\) shows why evaluations at the two binary points do not determine arbitrary higher coefficients. \(\square\)

There is also a useful exact upper comparison in characteristic two. Define \(M_{ij}=x_{ij}\prod_{k\ne j}(1-x_{ik})\). Then \(I_r=\det M\), by expanding the determinant: each permutation term is exactly the point indicator above. Thus a determinant circuit yields an indicator circuit by a polynomial-size constant-depth substitution. The reverse interpolation just proved may require a larger field. These are two distinct reductions, not an unqualified same-field equivalence.

Binary component depth gap

Corollary. No generic operation can take every polynomial-size constant-depth binary arithmetic circuit of polynomial degree and return any requested homogeneous component at polynomial size and bounded depth, uniformly over the requested degree. This already fails for \(G_n=\prod_{i=1}^n(1+x_i)\).

Proof. Its degree-\(q\) component is the elementary symmetric polynomial \(e_q\). Take \(q=2^a\) and \(n=2q-1\). At a Boolean vector of weight \(w\), \(e_q\) has value \(\binom wq\bmod2\). Writing the binary digits of \(w\), the identity \((1+t)^w=\prod_{b:w_b=1}(1+t^{2^b})\) in \(\mathbb F_2[t]\) shows that the coefficient of \(t^q\) is the \(a\)-th digit of \(w\). Since \(0\le w\le2q-1\), this is one exactly when \(w\ge q\): majority on an odd number of inputs. A uniform polynomial-size fixed-depth bound for these components contradicts the cited majority lower bound. This is an attributed consequence of that theorem and the displayed coefficient identity, not a new Boolean circuit lower bound. \(\square\)

The existing extension-field depth gap is consistent with this result: cheap large-field interpolation and free binary coefficient projection cannot be combined. The corollary does not exclude a special component extraction on a restricted circuit class with independently proved hypotheses.

Binary determinant depth gap

Corollary. Formal determinant polynomials over \(\mathbb F_2\) have no polynomial-size fixed-depth arithmetic circuits with binary constants. An explicit affine substitution reduces MOD3 to a sum of two determinants of order \(2n+3\), followed by addition of one.

Proof. Let \(A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), so \(A^3=I\), and put \(M_i=I+x_i(A-I)\). On a Boolean input of weight \(w\), \(M_1\cdots M_n=A^w\), whose trace is zero when \(3\mid w\) and one otherwise. It remains to realize its two diagonal entries as determinants at constant substitution depth.

Let \(T\) be the upper block-bidiagonal matrix with \(n+1\) diagonal blocks \(I_2\) and superdiagonal blocks \(-M_1,\ldots,-M_n\), numbered from zero. It has determinant one, and its inverse's block \((0,n)\) is \(M_1\cdots M_n\), by the finite geometric series for the strictly upper block matrix. For \(u=1,2\), let \(b_u\) be the standard vector of the last block in coordinate \(u\), and \(c_u\) the standard vector of the first block in coordinate \(u\). The block determinant identity gives \[\det\begin{pmatrix}T&b_u\\-c_u^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix} =c_u^{\mathsf T}T^{-1}b_u=(M_1\cdots M_n)_{uu}.\] This identity is formal because \(T^{-1}\) is the finite polynomial series and \(\det T=1\). The matrices have order \(2n+3\) and entries zero, one or affine functions of individual \(x_i\). Two polynomial-size fixed-depth binary determinant circuits would therefore give such a circuit for residue-zero MOD3 by adding their outputs and one. On Boolean inputs this contradicts Smolensky's Theorem 2. Odd matrix orders suffice for the contradiction; arbitrary padding by identity blocks preserves the usual family interpretation. No extension-field constant is used. \(\square\)
