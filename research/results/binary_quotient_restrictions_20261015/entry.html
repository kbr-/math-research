Quotient restriction data

Lemma. Fix integers \(v\ge0,d\ge1,k\ge0\), \(2k\le d\), put \(n=\max(v+1,6)\), and \(B=z^2+z\). Let \(\mathcal C_{v,d,k}\) consist of formal quotients \[ q(z,x)=g(z,x)/B^k,\qquad g=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\prod_j\ell_{ij}(z,x), \] where every supplied factor is binary affine, each term has at most d factors, and divisibility holds formally. Zero factors and empty products are allowed. Let E be the finite binary extension of least cardinality exceeding \(36dn\); write \(a=[E:\mathbb F_2]\), choose any binary basis, and choose \(U\subset E\) with \(|U|=36dn+1\).

There is one family of substitutions depending only on \(v,d\), preserving z and B, whose complete restricted quotient data distinguish every two members of \(\mathcal C_{v,d,k}\). Each datum is an E-polynomial in z and six scalar formal variables Y. Its a binary coefficient components have division-free unbounded-fanin binary arithmetic circuits of depth at most two and total gate/wire size \[ O\bigl(a(d+1)^7\bigr) \] per seed. All seeds together cost \(O(na(d+1)^8)\), with \(a=O(\log(dn+1))\). The data can be constructed by ordinary finite polynomial coefficient arithmetic on the restricted factor lists. No coefficient list for the unrestricted input polynomial is required. These bounds describe output circuits; they do not place that coefficient arithmetic in fixed-depth Boolean circuits.

Proof: homogenize only the x variables. Introduce \(X_0\). Replace a factor \(c+\epsilon z+\sum b_jx_j\) by \((c+\epsilon z)X_0+\sum b_jX_j\), and pad each term with \(X_0\) to have d factors. Add dummy X variables if needed to reach n. This gives \[ G(z,X)=X_0^d g(z,X_1/X_0,\ldots,X_v/X_0),\qquad Q=G/B^k=X_0^d q(z,X_1/X_0,\ldots,X_v/X_0). \] The displayed homogenizations are polynomials. Divisibility by B survives because it is coefficientwise in the x monomials and B uses z alone. Over \(E(z)\), G is a homogeneous sum of at most three degree-d products of linear forms in X. Its difference from any second such G is a sum of at most six such products. It has X-degree d and z-degree at most d; Q has z-degree at most \(d-2k\).

For each \(\beta\in U\), apply the source's r=6 substitution \(X_i\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^6\beta^{ij}Y_j\), indexing the n homogenizing/dummy variables by i from one to n. Extend it by fixing z. Call the images \(G_\beta,Q_\beta\). Then \[ G_\beta=B^kQ_\beta\quad\hbox{in }E[z,Y_1,\ldots,Y_6]. \] The imported theorem applies over the field \(E(z)\). The proof's bad-seed polynomial has at most \(36dn\) roots in that field, hence also at most that many among the constant seeds in E. If all data for q and \(\widetilde q\) agree, all images of \(G+\widetilde G\) vanish. Lemma 11 gives \(G=\widetilde G\), and specialization \(X_0=1\), followed by cancellation of nonzero B in a domain, gives \(q=\widetilde q\). This proves pairwise distinguishability, including the zero member. It does not give injectivity on the linear span of all such numerators.

Proof: complete data and costs. Each restricted factor has at most twelve monomials: six Y terms with coefficients affine in z. Expand its at most d factors in the fixed seven variables z,Y. Coefficient tables have polynomial size: all quotients have support among \[ z^tY^\alpha,\qquad 0\le t\le d-2k,\quad |\alpha|=d, \] at most \((d-2k+1)\binom{d+5}{5}=O((d+1)^6)\) monomials. Intermediate products have degree at most d in each of z and total Y, so ordinary sparse multiplication by one twelve-term factor, addition of three products, and univariate division by the known monic \(B^k\) for each Y monomial all take polynomially many E-operations on polynomial tables. This is a finite algorithm on explicit restricted factors, not a promise of a small coefficient extractor on an arbitrary implicit multivariate circuit.

Resolve each final E coefficient in the chosen binary basis. Each resulting component is an ordinary \(\mathbb F_2[z,Y]\) polynomial on that support. Write each nonzero monomial as a single product of at most \(2d\) ordinary variable wires, then sum them. Depth is at most two, and all a component circuits cost \(O(a(d+1)^7)\). A field element is never treated as a binary gate constant. The Y variables remain scalar indeterminates; arbitrary E-valued inputs encoded in a growing binary basis are not substituted into them. Multiplying by \(|U|=O(dn)\) proves the family bound. \(\square\)

Linear assembly gap

Proposition. For the fixed family of data above, at all sufficiently large \(d=4k\), \(v=d\), no \(\mathbb F_2\)-linear map from its full binary coefficient array to \(\mathbb F_2[z,x_1,\ldots,x_v]\) returns q for every member of \(\mathcal C_{v,d,k}\). This excludes only a universal linear assembly on the complete data, with coefficients and output polynomials fixed independently of the member. It does not exclude nonlinear circuit construction from the factor lists, a nonlinear decoder on promised data, or a q-dependent assembly.

Proof. Every subset \(A\subseteq[v]\) with \(|A|=2k=d/2\) gives the one-product numerator \[ g_A=B^k\prod_{i\in A}x_i, \qquad q_A=\prod_{i\in A}x_i. \] It uses exactly d binary affine factors, including k copies of both z and z+1. Its homogenized quotient is \(X_0^{2k}\prod_{i\in A}X_i\). Distinct \(q_A\)'s are linearly independent; there are \(\binom d{d/2}\ge2^d/(d+1)\) of them. The full binary coefficient array has dimension at most \[ L_d=a(36dn+1)(d-2k+1)\binom{d+5}{5}=O(d^8\log(d+1)),\qquad n=d+1. \] This conservative polynomial bound suffices. The image of any linear map from those arrays has dimension at most \(L_d\), smaller than \(\binom d{d/2}\) eventually. Such a map cannot contain all the \(q_A\). This argument concerns the full coefficient data, so weakening them to selected values cannot repair linear assembly. Each individual \(q_A\) already has a depth-one polynomial-size circuit. The contradiction therefore supplies no arithmetic circuit lower bound. \(\square\)
