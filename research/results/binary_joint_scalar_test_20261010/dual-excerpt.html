Canonical dual transport

Lemma (filtered dual transport). Let \(J\subseteq F[x]_{\le D}\) be a degree-\(D\) pseudoideal and put \(Q_k=F[x]_{\le k}/(J\cap F[x]_{\le k})\). A polynomial \(g\) of degree at most \(\delta\) defines \[m_{g,k}:Q_k\longrightarrow Q_{k+\delta},\qquad [f]\longmapsto[gf]\quad(k+\delta\le D),\] and its canonical dual is \[m_{g,k}^{\vee}:Q_{k+\delta}^*\longrightarrow Q_k^*,\qquad \lambda\longmapsto(f\mapsto\lambda(gf)).\tag{1}\] These maps have equal ranks. Their compositions reverse order with precisely the sum of the original degree shifts, and scalar extension preserves their ranks and kernels. No moment pairing or old polynomial representative of a dual vector is required.

Proof. Bounded multiplication closure sends \(J\cap F[x]_{\le k}\) into \(J\cap F[x]_{\le k+\delta}\), proving well-definedness of both maps. In compatible bases the second matrix is the transpose of the first, proving equal rank. Evaluating either side of the composition identity on \([f]\) gives \(\lambda(ghf)\); every intermediate product fits the summed cutoff. Finite-dimensional scalar extension preserves exact sequences and ranks, proving the remaining assertions. The inclusions \(Q_a\hookrightarrow Q_b\), for \(a\le b\), are injective because every quotient uses the same \(J\); their duals are restriction maps.

For binary Boolean inputs and the analysis phases \(u=1+\omega g\), \(v=1+\omega^2g\), the identity \(uv=1\) modulo Booleanity through \(2\delta\) yields \[m_{u,k}^{\vee}m_{v,k+\delta}^{\vee}:Q_{k+2\delta}^*\longrightarrow Q_k^*\] equal to restriction whenever \(k+2\delta\le D\). This is a composition between different filtered spaces, not an uncharged inverse endomorphism. Likewise a rectangular tuple map transposes to the sum of the coordinate dual maps; no Gram squaring is involved. \(\square\)

Boolean coefficient action. On the full Boolean algebra with monomial basis \(x^S\), let \(e_S\) denote the dual coefficient functional. Multiplication by \(x_i\) sends \(x^S\) to \(x^{S\cup\{i\}}\). Thus \[m_{x_i}^{\vee}e_S=\begin{cases}e_S+e_{S\setminus\{i\}},&i\in S,\\0,&i\notin S.\end{cases}\tag{2}\] This follows by testing each basis monomial. It is a number operator plus an annihilation operator; the integral creation/annihilation basis matrices reduce to this identity in characteristic two, with their ordering signs disappearing. On a quotient, only the combined map is automatically valid. For the Boolean ideal \((1+x)\), its annihilator is spanned by \(e_\varnothing+e_{\{1\}}\). The combined operator fixes it, whereas its two summands send it separately to \(e_\varnothing\) and \(e_{\{1\}}\), neither an annihilator. No separate ladder action on an arbitrary hard quotient is assumed.

The evaluation control from the preceding cycle is compatible with (1): evaluation is a cheap functional in the canonical dual, even when its moment-density representative has degree \(N\). Canonical duality removes that representation requirement, not a charged step from an established lower-bound construction. A dual witness must still yield a surviving old weight or a direct normalized source contradiction. It does not guarantee that evaluation annihilates the hard old equations or that one coherent source functional exists.

Reversibility does not remove exact value degree

Proposition (reversible value degree). Let \(\sigma\) be a nonidentity permutation matrix over any field, and suppose a permutation-valued computation on \(N\ge1\) Boolean bits has value \(\sigma\) exactly when all bits are one and identity otherwise. Its entrywise unique multilinear polynomial representation is \[I+(\sigma-I)\prod_{i=1}^N x_i.\tag{3}\] At least one entry has exact degree \(N\), regardless of the length or reversibility of the computation. The inverse-valued computation has the same property with \(\sigma^{-1}\).

Proof. The displayed matrix has precisely the required values, and multilinear interpolation on the Boolean cube is unique over every field. Since \(\sigma-I\ne0\), some top coefficient is nonzero. Replace \(\sigma\) by \(\sigma^{-1}\) for the inverse. This is an exact-value control: approximate proof interpretations remain allowed. It does not prove a lower bound against a branching program. \(\square\)
