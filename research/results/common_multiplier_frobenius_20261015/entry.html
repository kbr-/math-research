Proof when the multiplier is cheaper. If \(K\le e\), (1) is \(b+pK+e\le D\). Choose a prime-field basis \(u_1,\ldots,u_h\) of \(U\), and write a kernel vector as \(u=\sum_j c_j u_j\), \(c_j\in F\). Multiply \(w(\sum_i t_iE_i)u\in J_F\) by \(u^{p-1}\). Domain reduction gives

\[\sum_i t_i\sum_j c_j^p[wE_i u_j]=0\quad\text{in }Q_D\otimes F,\tag{3}\]

because \(u^p=\sum_j c_j^p u_j^p\) and each \(u_j^p-u_j\) has a domain certificate through \(pK\). All products fit \(b+e+pK\); \(w\) is never raised to the \(p\)-th power. Choose a prime-field basis of \(Q_D\). In any coordinate, (3) has the form \(\sum_i t_i h_i=0\), where \(h_i\in F^p\), since the coordinates of \([wE_i u_j]\) are in \(\mathbb F_p\). The elements \(t_1,\ldots,t_m\) are linearly independent over \(F^p=\mathbb F_p(t_1^p,\ldots,t_m^p)\). To see this directly, clear a common denominator in the \(p\)-th powers: monomials in the \(i\)-th summand have exponent vector congruent to the \(i\)-th unit vector modulo \(p\), disjoint for distinct \(i\). Hence every \(h_i=0\). Each coordinate now says \((\sum_j c_j v_{ij})^p=0\), where \(v_{ij}\in\mathbb F_p\) is its coordinate in \([wE_i u_j]\). A field has no nonzero element with zero \(p\)-th power, so \(\sum_j c_jv_{ij}=0\). This is \(wE_i u\in J_F\) for every \(i\). The converse kernel inclusion is a linear combination, proving (2).

Statement. For every \(e\ge1\), take binary variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_e,y_1,\ldots,y_e,u,v,z\), set \(X=\prod_i x_i\), \(Y=\prod_i y_i\), and \(D=e+1\). Let \(B_D\) be the Boolean domain-ideal part through \(D\), and set

\[J=B_D+\langle Xu+z,\ Xv,\ Yu,\ Yv+z\rangle_{\mathbb F_2}.\tag{4}\]

Then \(J\) is a proper degree-\(D\) pseudoideal. On \(U=\langle u,v\rangle\), weight one and actual errors \(X,Y\), the stacked rank is two but the generic scalar-combination rank is one. Thus a bare product reserve \(K+e\le D\) does not imply common-kernel equality, even for genuine Boolean polynomials. At \(K=1,p=2\), the new sufficient buffer is one degree above this counterexample.

Proof. Boolean reduction is degree-nonincreasing. The four extra generators have distinct squarefree top monomials \(Xu,Xv,Yu,Yv\), all of degree \(D\). No nonzero combination of them can reduce to degree below \(D\), and none can belong to \(B_D\). Hence every element of \(J\) of degree less than \(D\) belongs to \(B_D\); its allowed variable multiples remain Boolean consequences. This proves pseudoideal closure. Reduction also shows that neither one nor any nonzero linear combination of \(u,v,z\) belongs to \(J\). The two multiplication maps on \((u,v)\) are the row vectors \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\), with output in the surviving line \(\langle[z]\rangle\). Their stack has rank two; their generic combination \((t_1,t_2)\) has rank one, with spurious kernel vector \(t_2u+t_1v\). All products used are of degree \(e+1\). The zero assignment satisfies the generators, so this is a satisfiable control, not a hard unsatisfiable instance.

What the extra degree does. In the actual degree-\(e+2\) PC closure of these same generators, multiply \(Xu+z\) by \(u\) and use \(X(u^2+u)\) and the original relation to derive \(zu+z\). Multiplying \(Yv+z\) by \(u\), and \(Yu\) by \(v\), derives \(zu\); hence derive \(z\). All displayed degrees are at most \(e+2\). The four error products then vanish. Thus the counterexample does not persist after the theorem's additional degree is paid. It exposes precisely a truncated action, not a contradiction with simultaneous diagonalization in a full Boolean function algebra.
